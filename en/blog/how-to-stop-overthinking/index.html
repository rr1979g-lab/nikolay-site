How to stop overthinking and break the thought loop

Nikolay Shcherbakov · Published: 2026-11-26

You replay the same conversation, mistake or decision for the tenth time, and it still isn't any clearer. Here is why the mind gets stuck in loops, and the simple steps that help you step out without fighting yourself.

What overthinking and rumination really are

Rumination is when the same thought keeps coming back but leads nowhere. You go over what you said in a meeting, reread an old message, weigh a decision you have already made. Every new round promises that just a little more thinking will finally bring clarity. It doesn't, and each round leaves you a bit more tired.

It helps to tell this apart from ordinary thinking. Useful thinking moves forward: new facts appear, new options, a next step. Thoughts in circles stand still: the same arguments, the same “what if” and “I should have”, only more anxious each time.

Why we keep thinking in circles

Most often there is an understandable wish behind it: to find a final answer and feel certain. The mind believes that if it thinks everything through, the unpleasant feeling will go away. But many questions simply have no final answer: what a colleague really thinks of you, whether you did the right thing a year ago, what tomorrow will bring. Searching for something that isn't there is exactly what turns into an endless loop.

Other things feed it too: tiredness, lack of sleep, uncertainty, a habit of double-checking everything. Loops tend to get louder in the evening and at night, when there is nothing else to do. And they grow when we argue with them, because every counter-argument gives the thought new fuel.

How to stop overthinking: 6 steps

In his book Thoughts in Circles, Nikolay Shcherbakov puts the core idea simply: a thought doesn't need to be defeated, you can simply not continue it. In practice that becomes a short sequence:

  • Notice the loop. The moment you think “I'm going over this again” you are already out of autopilot.
  • Name what is happening: “this is replaying”, “this is that conversation again”. A label lets you see the thought from the outside.
  • Work out what you are facing: a fact, a question or a repetition. A fact can be accepted, a question can get an action, a repetition can be let go.
  • Answer once, or not at all. If the question can be solved, write down one concrete step. If it can't, tell yourself honestly: “there is no answer right now.”
  • Move to your next action: something physical and specific, like washing a cup, stepping outside or replying to an email.
  • When the thought comes back (it will), don't scold yourself. Repeat the same steps; each time it takes a little less effort.

A real-life example

Say you gave an awkward answer to your manager in a meeting, and all evening you keep replaying the scene. Step one: “Stop, I'm replaying the meeting again.” Step two: “This is a repetition; there are no new facts here.” Step three: what is the fact? The answer came out badly, and that has already happened. Is there a question you can answer with an action? Yes: tomorrow you can send a short follow-up message.

Write it down in one line: “Tomorrow at 10:00, send two sentences clarifying my point.” That's it, you have answered once. Then move on: clear the table, call a friend, cook dinner. If the scene pops up again half an hour later, you already know what to do: notice it, name it and don't continue it. The thought gets quieter not because you beat it, but because you stopped feeding it.

Exercises that help right now

Once a loop is running, logical arguments rarely help. It works better to move your attention out of your head and into your body and the present moment:

  • Three slow exhales. Notice the air leaving and make each exhale a little longer than the inhale. For more techniques, see our article on breathing exercises for calm.
  • “5 things around me.” Silently name five objects you can see and three sounds you can hear.
  • Scheduled worry time. Tell yourself: “I'll think about this at 6 pm for 15 minutes.” When the time comes, there often turns out to be nothing left to think about.
  • Put it on paper. Write the thought down in one sentence, and next to it one step you could take. Paper stops repetition better than your head does.
  • Change the setting. A short walk or a simple task with your hands often breaks the loop faster than any amount of thinking.

How to turn it into a habit

Stepping out of a loop once is easy; doing it every day is harder. Short, regular pauses help: a few times a day, stop for a minute, feel your breathing and notice where your attention is right now — in the past, in the future, or here.

What not to do

  • Don't try to forbid yourself from thinking about something; a suppressed thought usually comes back stronger.
  • Don't look for a final answer where there isn't one. Sometimes an honest “I don't know” is the best answer.
  • Don't keep asking friends or family for reassurance about the same thing; the relief is short and a new loop follows.
  • Don't save big questions for the middle of the night. If a thought shows up before bed, write it down and come back to it in the morning.

When to talk to a professional

If intrusive thoughts take up most of your day, disrupt your sleep, work or relationships, or come with strong anxiety, low mood or loss of interest in life, please talk to a psychologist, therapist or doctor. Questions and answers

How do I stop thinking about the same thing over and over?

Notice the loop, name it and decide whether it is a fact, a question or a repetition. Answer once or not at all, then switch to a concrete action. When the thought returns, calmly repeat the same steps.

Why do my thoughts race at night?

In the evening there are fewer tasks and distractions, and tiredness makes worry louder. Writing the thought down, postponing it until morning and taking a few slow exhales can help.

Can I just stop myself from thinking about it?

Usually not: a suppressed thought tends to come back stronger. What works better is not arguing with the thought and not continuing it, but bringing your attention back to what is happening now.

What is a quick exercise to stop overthinking?

Try three slow exhales followed by “5 things around me”: name five objects you can see and three sounds you can hear. This moves your attention out of your head and into the present moment.

When is overthinking a reason to see a doctor?

When it takes up most of your day, disrupts sleep, work or relationships, or comes with strong anxiety or low mood. In that case, please reach out to a psychologist, therapist or doctor.
