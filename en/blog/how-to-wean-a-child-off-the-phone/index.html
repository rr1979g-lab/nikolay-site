How to get your child off the phone: a plan by age

Nikolay Shcherbakov · Published: 2026-11-25

Taking the phone away is easy. Helping a child reach for it less on their own is much harder. Here is why kids get hooked on screens, what works at different ages and how to bring in rules without a fight every evening.

Why kids find it so hard to put the phone down

Games, short videos and messaging apps are designed to hold attention: there is always a next level, a next clip, a new message. Adults struggle with this too, and a child's ability to stop and switch tasks is still developing. So the conclusion "my kid just has no willpower" is usually wrong. The phone simply has a huge head start over everything else competing for their attention.

Long hours on the phone often hide something else: boredom, tiredness after school, wanting to keep up with friends, worry or loneliness. If you just take the phone away, that need doesn't disappear; it only changes shape. A good plan is not only about limits but also about what fills the time you free up.

Start by looking at your own habits

Children copy behaviour, not words. If adults scroll at dinner and fall asleep with a phone in hand, the rule "get off your devices" sounds hollow. Before you set limits for your child, spend a couple of days honestly noticing your own phone habits.

It helps to agree on rules for the whole family: no phones at the table, phones charge overnight outside the bedroom, one shared screen-free hour a day. Toddlers and preschoolers (2–6): replace, don't just ban

At this age the phone often appears as a quick soother: in a queue, at a café, when a parent needs ten quiet minutes. Those are exactly the situations to change first. The less the screen is tied to tears and tantrums, the easier it is to step away from it.

What helps:

  • Keep a small "go bag": a book, crayons, stickers, a little toy.
  • Make screen time a rare, predictable event rather than the answer to every "I'm bored".
  • Watch cartoons together and talk about them, so the screen becomes a shared activity, not a babysitter.
  • Give a warning before it ends — "one more episode, then we switch off" — and then calmly follow through.
  • No phone before sleep or during meals.

Primary school kids (7–11): clear rules and rest time

This is often when a child gets a first phone, and it is the best moment to agree on rules. Changing established habits later is much harder. Sit down together and decide when the phone can be used and when it stays aside: during homework, at meals, an hour before bed and overnight. Agree which apps are allowed and how to ask for a new one.

Teenagers (12+): agreement instead of control

With teenagers, hard bans often backfire: they find a way around the limits and trust suffers. At this age the phone is also the main channel for friendships, so switching it off completely feels like being cut off.

A different approach works better: talk about what an endless feed does to attention and sleep, and look for a solution together. Invite your teen to notice how much time goes on social media and choose what they would like to change. Rules they helped write are rules they are more likely to keep. Make some of them shared, too: phones out of the bedroom at night applies to adults as well. Our article on how to stop scrolling your phone has ideas a teen can try on their own.

Example of a family phone agreement

So that rules aren't forgotten or renegotiated every evening, write them down and put the sheet somewhere visible. Keep each rule short and concrete rather than vague like "use the phone less". Here is an example you can adapt:

  • On school days the phone comes after homework and chores, for the agreed amount of time.
  • Nobody has a phone at the table, adults included.
  • An hour before bed, all phones go to charge in the hallway or kitchen.
  • New apps and games are installed only after we talk about them together.
  • At weekends, one family hour or walk with no screens at all.
  • Once a month we sit down and decide what is working and what to change.

What to offer instead of the phone

If you remove the screen and offer nothing in its place, a child will simply wait for the phone to come back. The freed-up time needs filling, ideally together.

  • Walks, sports and clubs, where a child moves and talks to people face to face.
  • Board games, building sets, drawing, cooking together.
  • Reading aloud for younger kids and a shared reading hour for the whole family.
  • Household jobs with a clear purpose, so the child feels their contribution matters.
  • A bit of real boredom: that is often where their own ideas and games come from.

How to bring in limits without constant fights

  • Change gradually: cut time step by step, not overnight.
  • Explain the reason — "the phone keeps you from falling asleep", not "because I said so".
  • Warn in advance before screen time ends.
  • Keep your word: a rule that is easy to beg your way out of stops working.
  • Praise what goes well, and avoid making the phone the main punishment or reward.
  • Review the rules every few months as your child grows.

When to ask a professional

This article is about family habits, not medical or psychological advice. If your child reacts to limits with intense aggression or despair, withdraws from friends, or their sleep, schoolwork or mood clearly get worse, talk to a paediatrician or a child psychologist. Apps can help you keep rules, but they don't replace professional help or a parent's real attention.

Questions and answers

How do I get my child off the phone without tantrums?

Reduce time gradually, give a warning before screen time ends and offer something interesting instead. When rules are the same every day, there are far fewer arguments.

Should I just take the phone away?

It can work as a short-term measure, but without an explanation and an alternative a child sees it as punishment and simply waits to get the phone back. Clear, agreed rules work more reliably.

How do I get a teenager to spend less time on the phone?

Instead of bans, talk about what is actually getting in the way — sleep, school, focus — and write the rules together. Make some limits apply to the whole family.

How can a parental control app help?

It supports the rules you have agreed on: PARENT LOCK lets you set rules for phone use, choose allowed apps and set rest time. But start with a conversation, not the settings.

At what age should a child get their own phone?

There is no single right age: decide based on real need and your child's maturity. What matters most is agreeing on rules the same day the phone arrives.
