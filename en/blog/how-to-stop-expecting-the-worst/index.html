How to stop expecting the worst and worrying about the future

Nikolay Shcherbakov · Published: 2026-11-28

Nothing has happened yet, but you have already lived through the bad outcome in vivid detail. Here is where the habit of expecting the worst comes from, and the simple steps that bring you back from the anxious movie in your head to the day you are actually in.

What expecting the worst looks like

Your boss writes “Come and see me” and you are already rehearsing the conversation where you lose your job. Someone you love doesn’t pick up the phone and you run through the most frightening reasons. A letter arrives from the bank and you start counting debts that may not even exist. Your body reacts as if the trouble has already happened: a knot in your stomach, a racing heart, a sleepless night.

The frustrating part is that, most of the time, nothing bad happens. Your boss wanted to talk about a project, your loved one was driving, the letter was an advert. But the day has already been spent in worry. Nikolay Shcherbakov writes about exactly this in his book What If Everything Turns Out Well?: we pay with today for a future that doesn’t exist yet.

Why we always expect the worst

The mind tries to prepare for danger in advance. When the future is uncertain, it fills the gap with the most unpleasant option, as if imagining the worst will make it hurt less when it comes. In reality we simply live through the trouble twice: first in our heads, and sometimes again in real life.

Tiredness, poor sleep, a steady stream of alarming news and past experience all make the habit stronger. If something once went wrong, the mind starts expecting a repeat everywhere. And uncertainty feels like a threat, even though it works both ways: the unknown can hold good things, not only bad ones.

How to stop expecting the worst: 6 steps

The goal is not to force yourself to think only positive thoughts. It is to learn to notice an anxious scenario and come back to reality. Here is a sequence you can use in almost any situation:

  • Notice the movie. Catch the moment a thought about the future becomes a story with a bad ending: “he’s going to say…”, “it will all fall apart…”, “I won’t cope…”.
  • Name it: “I’m writing the worst-case script again.” A label helps you look at the thought from the outside instead of living inside it.
  • Separate facts from guesses. Write down what you know for sure, and separately what you are assuming. Usually the facts take two or three lines, and the rest is guesswork.
  • Ask the opposite question: “What if everything turns out well?” Picture at least one realistic good outcome. This isn’t self-deception, just honesty: the future is unknown in both directions.
  • Find what you can influence today. One small action — making a call, asking a question, getting documents ready — relieves more anxiety than an hour of thinking.
  • Come back to the present. Take a few slow breaths out, feel the ground under your feet and turn to whatever is in front of you right now.

An example: the “we need to talk” message

In the evening your manager messages you: “Please come by at 9 tomorrow, we need to talk.” Five minutes later you are recalling every mistake you made this year and wondering where to look for a new job. Step one: “Stop, I’m filming a movie with a bad ending again.” Step two: what are the facts? There is a meeting at 9 tomorrow. That’s all — the topic is unknown.

Step three: what other outcomes are possible? A new project, a question about a report, planning holidays, a request to help a colleague. Step four: what can you do today? Jot down a few lines about what you are working on, so you can talk calmly in the morning. Then dinner, a walk, sleep. If the anxious picture comes back, you know the routine: notice it, name it, return to the facts. The morning may turn out nothing like the one your mind painted.

Everyday exercises

Expecting the worst is a habit, and habits change through small, regular steps. Try a few of these:

  • Two columns. When you feel anxious, split a page into “facts” and “guesses”. It takes two minutes and quickly shrinks the problem.
  • Three outcomes. For any worrying situation, write down a bad, a good and the most likely outcome. The most likely one is often ordinary and boring.
  • Check the past. Remember three times you expected the worst. How did they really end? A list like this makes anxious predictions easier to doubt.
  • A breathing pause. Make each breath out a little longer than the breath in, and repeat a few times. For more techniques, see our article on breathing exercises for calm.
  • An evening question. Before bed, ask yourself: “What went better today than I expected?” It trains your attention to notice the good, not only the risks.

How breathing, a book and an app can help

Once anxiety has taken hold, the body often works better than logic. A few minutes of slow, rhythmic breathing gives you a pause between an anxious thought and your reaction to it. The hard part is keeping a steady rhythm without counting in your head. That is why the author created the free Android app Breath Prana Moment: it includes Box Breathing, 4-7-8, Equal and Coherent Breathing, custom sessions, voice guidance and progress statistics.

If you want to understand the habit of living through bad outcomes in advance more deeply, read What If Everything Turns Out Well?. It teaches you to tell a real problem from an anxious scenario, to return to the facts and to notice good possibilities, and it includes short practices for working with thoughts, attention, the body and breathing. And if your mind loops over the past rather than the future, read how to stop overthinking.

What not to do

  • Don’t force yourself to “think positive” — strained optimism breaks easily and adds guilt.
  • Don’t seek relief through endless checking: rereading news, searching symptoms online or reading messages over and over helps for a minute, then the worry returns.
  • Don’t argue with an anxious thought for hours — every new argument feeds it. Name it and move to an action instead.
  • Don’t make important decisions at the peak of anxiety. Wait for the wave to pass and come back to the question with a clearer head.

When to talk to a professional

If worry about the future takes up most of your day, gets in the way of sleep, work and relationships, or comes with panic attacks, a pounding heart or low mood, please talk to a psychologist, therapist or doctor. The advice in this article, the book and the app are tools for everyday life; they do not replace professional help or treatment. During breathing exercises, breathe gently; if you feel dizzy or uncomfortable, stop and return to normal breathing.

Questions and answers

How do I stop always expecting the worst?

Notice when a thought turns into an anxious scenario, separate facts from guesses and ask yourself, “What if everything turns out well?” Then pick one action for today and come back to the present.

Why do I always think something bad will happen?

The mind tries to prepare for danger and fills uncertainty with the most unpleasant option. Tiredness, poor sleep, alarming news and past bad experiences make the habit stronger.

How can I calm anxiety about the future right now?

Take a few slow breaths, making each exhale a little longer than the inhale, and split a page into “facts” and “guesses”. This quickly shrinks the worry and brings you back to reality.

Does positive thinking help with anxiety?

Forced optimism rarely works. It helps more to admit honestly that the future is unknown in both directions and to picture at least one realistic good outcome alongside the bad one.

When is worry about the future a reason to see a doctor?

When it disrupts sleep, work and relationships, takes up most of your day, or comes with panic attacks and low mood. In that case, talk to a psychologist, therapist or doctor.
